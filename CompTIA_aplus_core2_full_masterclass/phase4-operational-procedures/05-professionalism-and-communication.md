# 05 — Professionalism, Communication & Privacy

**Domain:** 4.0 Operational Procedures (21%)
**Maps to objective:** 4.7 — Given a scenario, use proper communication techniques and professionalism; 4.6 — data privacy/licensing concepts

## Communication techniques (heavily scenario-tested)

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use clear, concise, non-technical language with end users | Use jargon/acronyms the user won't understand |
| Actively listen — let the user finish explaining | Interrupt or dismiss the user's description as wrong |
| Be culturally sensitive and use appropriate professional titles | Be dismissive of language barriers or unfamiliar customs |
| Maintain a positive attitude, avoid arguing with a customer | Minimize the problem or act rushed with the user |
| Set and meet expectations, communicate timelines/status | Make promises you can't keep, or go silent on a ticket |
| Avoid distractions — don't take personal calls during a client interaction | Multitask visibly in front of the customer |
| If you need to step away, explain why and offer to help find someone else | Just walk away without explanation |

## Difficult customer scenarios

- **Do not argue or become defensive** — de-escalate, focus on the problem
- **Avoid being judgmental** about how the user caused the issue
- If a conflict can't be resolved at your level, **escalate appropriately** rather than continuing to argue

## Dealing with prohibited content/activity (a distinct, serious scenario type)

If a technician discovers illegal content or activity on a device during work (e.g., evidence of a crime):
1. **Do not act on personal judgment** — don't delete it, don't confront the user, don't investigate further yourself
2. **Preserve the evidence** — don't power off or alter the system if instructed to preserve chain of custody
3. **Report through proper channels** — follow your organization's established chain of custody / incident reporting procedure, which typically involves legal/management, and potentially law enforcement
4. **Document everything** — what was found, when, by whom, and who was notified

**Exam trap:** the "correct" answer in these scenarios is almost never "confront the user" or "delete it" — it's always "report it through the proper chain of custody / escalate to a supervisor," preserving evidence integrity.

## Licensing & data privacy concepts

| Concept | Notes |
|---|---|
| **EULA** (End User License Agreement) | Legal terms governing software use — violating it is a licensing compliance issue |
| **DRM** (Digital Rights Management) | Technical controls restricting copying/use of licensed content |
| **Open-source vs proprietary licensing** | Open source generally allows modification/redistribution under license terms; proprietary restricts this |
| **Personally Identifiable Information (PII)** | Data that can identify a specific person — name, SSN, address, etc. — requires careful handling |
| **Protected Health Information (PHI)** | Health-related data covered under regulations like HIPAA — extra-strict handling requirements |
| **Payment Card Industry (PCI) data** | Credit card data — governed by PCI-DSS compliance standards |
| **GDPR** | EU regulation on personal data privacy/handling — relevant if a business serves EU customers |

**Exam trap:** technicians who encounter PII/PHI/PCI data during routine support should handle it according to organizational policy and regulatory requirements, not just their own judgment — mishandling this data can create real legal liability for the organization.

## Quick self-check
1. What is the correct first move if a technician discovers illegal content on a customer's device?
2. What communication mistake is most commonly tested as "what should the technician have NOT done" in a difficult-customer scenario?
3. What's the difference between PII and PHI?
4. Why should a technician avoid using heavy technical jargon with an end user?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Do not act on it personally — preserve the evidence and report it through the proper chain of custody/escalation procedure (management, legal, potentially law enforcement).
2. Arguing with, dismissing, or being judgmental toward the customer instead of staying professional and de-escalating.
3. PII = information that can identify a specific person generally (name, SSN, address); PHI = specifically health-related information covered under regulations like HIPAA.
4. Because the goal is clear communication the user can actually understand and act on — jargon creates confusion and frustration rather than helping resolve the issue.
</details>
